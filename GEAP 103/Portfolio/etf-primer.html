Entry 001 — First Principles

An ETF is a basket of investments you buy with one trade.

Instead of picking one company and hoping, an exchange-traded fund holds many — sometimes thousands — and trades on an exchange like a single stock. The two things that matter most are what's inside the basket, and what it costs to hold it. This page walks through both, plus a quiz, a basket builder, and a glossary, so you can go from curious to informed.

Nothing here is personal financial advice — think of it as the reference sheet you'd want before your first conversation with an advisor or your first trade.

Entry 002 — Why ETFs Exist

What actually makes an ETF an ETF

Most ETFs are built to track an index — a fixed list of holdings like "the 500 largest U.S. companies" or "every investment-grade government bond." Rather than a manager guessing which stocks will win, the fund simply owns what's on the list, in proportion to the index's rules.

Because there's no team of analysts trying to beat the market, index ETFs are cheap to run — often a fraction of a percent per year — and that cost gets passed on to you as a lower fee, called the MER or expense ratio.

They also trade all day on an exchange, like a stock, which means you can buy or sell anytime the market is open, at whatever price it's trading for that moment.

Entry 003 — ETF vs. Mutual Fund

The short version

Diversification: one ETF can spread your money across hundreds or thousands of holdings, so a single company's bad quarter barely moves the needle.

Cost: broad-market index ETFs typically charge far less per year than actively managed mutual funds, and that gap compounds enormously over decades — see the ledger above.

Transparency: most ETFs publish their full holdings daily, so you always know what you own.

Trade-off: you give up the (unlikely) chance that a star manager beats the market, in exchange for reliably capturing the market's own return, minus a very small fee.

Entry 007 — Glossary

Terms worth knowing

MER / Expense Ratio
The annual fee, as a percentage of your investment, charged to run the fund. Lower is generally better, all else equal.
NAV
Net Asset Value — the per-share value of everything the fund owns, calculated once a day.
Tracking error
How closely a fund's actual return matches the index it's supposed to follow. Smaller is better.
Diversification
Spreading money across many holdings so no single one can do outsized damage.
Distribution yield
The income (dividends or interest) a fund has recently paid out, shown as a percentage of its price.
Rebalancing
Periodically buying or selling to bring your allocation back to your target mix.
Ticker symbol
The short code a fund trades under on an exchange — e.g. four letters you'd type into a brokerage.
Index
A defined, rules-based list of holdings — like "the 500 largest U.S. public companies" — that a fund can be built to track.
Entry 008 — Getting Started

A general starting sequence

Open a brokerage account

Any regulated broker that lists ETFs will work — compare trading fees and account minimums.

Choose the right account type

Tax-advantaged accounts (like a 401(k)/IRA in the U.S. or a TFSA/RRSP in Canada) often make sense before a regular taxable account, depending on your situation.

Set a target allocation

Use your time horizon and risk comfort — like the quiz above — to sketch a stocks-to-bonds split.

Favor low, transparent costs

Compare the MER of similar funds; over decades, small differences in fees compound into large differences in outcome.

Automate contributions

Regular investing on a schedule removes guesswork about timing the market.

Rebalance occasionally

Once or twice a year, nudge your holdings back toward your target mix.

Stay the course

Markets move in both directions; the historical edge in index investing has come from time in the market, not timing it.
